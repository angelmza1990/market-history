Market History

Methodology

Where the numbers come from, how they are calculated, and what they leave out.

Sources

  • Prices: daily closes from Yahoo Finance for indices, exchange-traded funds, futures and crypto. The ticker for every asset is listed in the summary table on the overview and in the performance table on the sectors page.
  • Economic series: FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Consumer prices (CPIAUCSL, CPILFESL), the effective federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS), 2- and 10-year Treasury yields (GS2, GS10), the unemployment rate (UNRATE), real GDP growth (A191RL1Q225SBEA quarterly, A191RL1A225NBEA annual), M2 (M2SL) and the NBER recession indicator (USREC).
  • Volatility: the CBOE VIX index, from Yahoo Finance.

Every source file is kept in the repository under data/raw/, so the site can be rebuilt even if a source changes. A scheduled job refreshes the data every weekday.

Returns

  • Calendar-year return = last close of the year ÷ last close of the previous year − 1.
  • A series' first year counts only if the series has a close in the previous year, so a partial first year is never shown as a full one. The current year is shown as year to date and is excluded from counts, averages, best and worst years.
  • Price return for stock indices, futures, the dollar index and crypto: dividends are not included. Over long periods dividends add materially; for the S&P 500 they have added roughly 1.5 to 2 percentage points a year since 2000, and more before that.
  • Total return for ETFs (sectors, bond funds, emerging markets, Argentina) and for the DAX, which is itself a performance index: dividends and coupons are reinvested.
  • Annualized return over n years = (end ÷ start)1/n − 1.
  • All figures are in the currency of the index (US dollars for US assets, euros for the Euro Stoxx 50 and DAX, yen for the Nikkei, and so on), and nominal: they are not adjusted for inflation unless labeled as such.

Risk

  • Drawdown: the percentage below the highest close reached so far, measured on daily closes from the start of each series. The overview and long-run pages show the deepest one and when it was recovered.
  • Volatility: standard deviation of monthly returns, multiplied by √12.
  • Beta and correlation: from monthly returns against the SPDR S&P 500 ETF over the last 120 months, or the whole history when shorter.

Economic groupings

  • A recession year is one with at least one month marked as recession by the NBER.
  • Inflation for a year is the December-to-December change in the CPI.
  • Fed policy compares the December average federal funds rate with the previous December's.
  • The yield curve going in is the 10-year minus 2-year yield in the December before the year starts, so it is known in advance.

These groupings describe history. With a few dozen years per group, a single year can move an average by several points, and none of them is a trading rule.

Valuation

  • Companies: the S&P 500 constituents and their GICS sectors, from Wikipedia's list of S&P 500 companies, one row per company (Class A shares for Alphabet, Fox and News Corp).
  • P/E = month-end close ÷ the sum of the last four quarterly earnings per share, as reported in Yahoo Finance's earnings history. Each quarter counts from its report date, so no month uses figures that were not yet public. Four reports must span three quarters; a gap leaves the months empty. Negative earnings, and multiples above 1,000, are not meaningful and are left out.
  • EV/EBITDA = (month-end close × shares outstanding + total debt − cash and short-term investments) ÷ EBITDA of the last twelve months, from Yahoo Finance's annual and quarterly statements, using normalized EBITDA when it is available. Each period counts from the first earnings report after it ends. Yahoo publishes about four years of statements, so the series starts in 2022. Not computed for the Financials sector.
  • Own history: today's value is ranked among the monthly values of the last 120 months (at least 36 for P/E, 24 for EV/EBITDA). The usual range is the 20th to the 80th percentile.
  • Sector: today's value divided by the median of the companies in the same sector with a meaningful value. Sector medians over time use the current members of the index, so companies that left it are missing from the past.
  • Signal: each comparison votes −1 (in the cheapest 20% of its history, or below 0.8× the sector median), +1 (in the most expensive 20%, or above 1.25×) or 0. The average of the votes is the score: −0.5 or below is undervalued, +0.5 or above overvalued, anything between fairly valued.
  • Forward P/E = latest close ÷ analysts' EPS estimate for the next twelve months; PEG = P/E ÷ expected yearly EPS growth over five years, as published by Yahoo Finance. Neither enters the signal.
  • Dividend yield = dividends paid in the last twelve months ÷ month-end close, since 2005. Free cash flow yield = free cash flow of the last twelve months ÷ market value, since 2022, not computed for Financials. For yields a high value is the cheap end.
  • Quality: each company is ranked within its sector on operating margin, return on equity and net debt ÷ EBITDA (lower is better); Financials on return on equity, return on assets and net margin. The average rank puts it in the top (high), middle (average) or bottom third (low). Figures are Yahoo Finance's for the last twelve months.
  • Prices are refreshed every weekday. Earnings and statements are refreshed when a company reports, or at least once a week.

Multiples ignore growth, the quality of earnings and where a company is in its cycle, so the signal describes price relative to the past and to peers, not what a business is worth.

Known limitations

  • Commodities use the front-month futures contract as reported by Yahoo Finance, without adjustment for rolling between contracts, and start in 2000. Days with a zero or negative price (WTI on April 20, 2020) are left out of return and drawdown calculations.
  • Index histories are limited to what Yahoo Finance provides: the Dow Jones from 1992, the Euro Stoxx 50 from 2007, the Nasdaq Composite from 1971.
  • Crypto trades every day; its history starts in September 2014 (Bitcoin) and November 2017 (Ethereum).
  • Argentina is represented by the Global X MSCI Argentina ETF in US dollars, which avoids the distortion of peso inflation but holds some companies listed abroad.

Reproduce it

The code is on GitHub. scripts/fetch_data.py downloads the sources and scripts/build_data.py builds the datasets the pages read. The build checks the S&P 500 against known calendar-year returns and stops if they do not match.

Portfolio check

  • Weights are scaled to add up to 100%. Repeated tickers are merged.
  • Past behavior assumes the weights are restored at the end of every month and uses month-end total returns, with dividends reinvested (gold and crypto are prices); trading costs and taxes are left out. It is compared with a chosen benchmark fund, also with dividends: SPY (S&P 500), QQQ (Nasdaq-100), DIA (Dow Jones), IWM (Russell 2000), EEM (emerging markets), or 60% SPY and 40% AGG (US bonds) rebalanced monthly. It starts when the youngest holding and the benchmark both have prices.
  • Past crises: the change from the month-end before each fall to the month-end at its low. A holding that was not trading yet is estimated as its beta against the S&P 500 (from all its monthly history, 1 if under two years) times the S&P 500's change.
  • Weighted P/E and EV/EBITDA are harmonic averages over the individual stocks with positive earnings or EBITDA: total weight divided by the sum of weight ÷ multiple. The S&P 500 figure weights companies by market value the same way.
  • Sector weights spread SPY across the index's sectors by market value; sector funds count toward their sector.
  • Beta and correlation use monthly returns against the S&P 500; volatility is the standard deviation of monthly returns × √12.
